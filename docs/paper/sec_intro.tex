\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{Motivation.} Thin composite panels stiffen as they vibrate. Mid-plane
stretching makes the restoring force of a bending mode cubic in its amplitude, so the
resonance frequency drifts upward with amplitude along the \emph{backbone curve}
$\Om(a)$ \cite{touze,shaw}. Under harmonic forcing, the hardening resonance leans over,
folds, and \emph{jumps} between coexisting branches with hysteresis. For a resonant
sensor the drift is a calibration error. For a harvester or an actuator the jump is a
loss of function. In practice, the designer of such a panel is not free to move its
linear frequency (set by the application) or its mass (set by the vehicle). The design
question is therefore whether the \emph{nonlinear} stiffness can be changed
\emph{independently} of both.

\subsection{Related work and positioning}\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Reduction and design of nonlinear normal modes.} The backbone is the
frequency--amplitude relation of a nonlinear normal mode \cite{shaw,kerschen2009,nayfeh1979}.
Spectral submanifold (SSM) theory makes it the reduced dynamics on the unique smoothest
invariant manifold tangent to a modal subspace \cite{haller2016}. Its parametrization can
be computed directly from finite-element models to arbitrary order
\cite{ponsioen2018,jain2022,vizzaccaro2022,opreni2023}; see \cite{touze} for a review of
geometrically nonlinear reduction. This makes nonlinear response a \emph{designable}
quantity. Dou et al.\ shaped normal-form coefficients of beams and frames by adjoint
shape optimization \cite{dou2015,doujensen2016}. Li derived explicit SSM sensitivities
\cite{li2024}. Pozzi et al.\ tailored conservative backbones through the reduced dynamics of
Lyapunov subcenter manifolds \cite{pozzi2024lsm}, obtained adjoint sensitivities of SSM
backbone curves at arbitrary order \cite{pozzi2025}, and optimized the topology of the
hardening/softening coefficient \cite{pozzi2025smo}. Liang et al.\ extended adjoint SSM
sensitivities to forced-response curves and density-based topology optimization
\cite{liang2025}. Force-based reduced-order models have also been used to optimize
nonlinear finite-element models \cite{xiao2026}. In all of these the design variables are
\emph{shape}, \emph{size} or \emph{material density} of isotropic structures, and the
question is how to reach a target response, with the frequency held alongside it by an
equality target on the backbone frequency at a prescribed amplitude \cite{pozzi2025} or a
tolerance constraint on the linear frequency \cite{liang2025}. None of them uses
a fiber-orientation field, and none imposes the curvature limit of automated fiber
placement.

\paragraph{Variable-stiffness composites.} Steering tows along curvilinear paths was
introduced for buckling and stress concentration \cite{hyer1991}. It is now a mature design
space for \emph{linear} objectives: in-plane stiffness and buckling
\cite{gurdal,lopes2008,ijsselmuiden}, and fundamental frequency through lamination
parameters \cite{abdalla2007,tsai1968}. It has also been optimized for a geometrically
nonlinear \emph{static} objective, postbuckling stiffness \cite{wu2013,raju2018}, where the
gain is attributed to the in-plane redistribution of load toward the supported edges
\cite{gurdal,wu2013}; the relaxed-stretch mechanism identified here is closely
analogous to that redistribution. Orientation is also optimized pointwise in
topology-optimization frameworks \cite{stegmann2005,nomura2015}, and continuous-fiber
printing \cite{matsuzaki2016} with toolpath-integrated, curvature-constrained design
\cite{wong2026} now makes such fields manufacturable. The \emph{nonlinear} vibration of
variable-stiffness plates has been \emph{analyzed}: Akhavan and Ribeiro computed the linear modes
\cite{akhavan2011} and the forced periodic vibrations \cite{akhavan2015,akhavan2017}, and
Ribeiro the nonlinear free vibrations \cite{ribeiro2012} (see also \cite{ribeiroakhavan2012}), of plates with \emph{prescribed}
curvilinear fiber paths, Houmat analyzed nonlinear free vibration with curvilinear fibers
\cite{houmat2013}, and Yan et al.\ built a semi-analytical framework for parametric studies
of nonlinear free and forced response \cite{yan2021}. Farsadi et al.\ optimized curvilinear
fiber paths for the \emph{linear} fundamental frequency and then examined the
amplitude-dependent nonlinear frequency of the optimum \cite{farsadi2021,farsadi2025}. To
our knowledge the hardening itself has not been the design objective, and the reason why a
fiber field can or cannot change it has not been identified.

\paragraph{Why it matters.} Designers of resonant devices already exploit or cancel
geometric nonlinearity by tuning design parameters, for example by balancing mechanical
hardening against electrostatic softening to extend the linear range of nanomechanical
sensors \cite{kacem2009}. A composite panel offers an additional freedom
that leaves geometry, mass and material unchanged. This paper asks whether that freedom
acts on the nonlinearity at all, and why.

\paragraph{What is new here.} Relative to the SSM-optimization literature, the design
variable is a manufacturable fiber-orientation field under the tow-curvature constraint,
at fixed mass and an exactly held linear frequency (an equality, as in the backbone
targets of \cite{pozzi2025}, but with the material fixed and only the orientation free). Relative to the
variable-stiffness literature, the objective is the nonlinear backbone, and the result is
not only an optimum but a \emph{mechanism}. A local Fourier theory predicts when steering
must help, where the fibers go and what limits the gain, and each prediction is tested
against the competing laminate freedoms: a uniform angle and the stacking sequence.

\subsection{Question, hypothesis and predictions}\label{sec:qh}
\paragraph{Scientific question.}
\begin{quote}\itshape
For a thin composite panel of fixed mass and fixed linear frequency, can the
\emph{spatial distribution} of fiber orientation reduce the geometric hardening of its
fundamental mode? If so, what physical mechanism provides the lever, what bounds it,
and how does it compare with the classical laminate freedoms --- a uniform ply angle and
the stacking sequence?
\end{quote}

\paragraph{Hypothesis.} (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}.) The linear frequency and the hardening are integrals of the
\emph{same} rotated stiffness tensor against two \emph{different} strain fields of the
fundamental mode. The frequency is set by the bending curvature,
$\omega_1^2=\int\bm\kappa^{\!\top}\bm D(\theta)\bm\kappa$. The hardening is set by the
membrane stretch the mode induces after the in-plane field has relaxed,
$\gamma=\tfrac12\int\bm\varepsilon_r^{\!\top}\bm A(\theta)\bm\varepsilon_r$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:model}). The two strain fields differ from point to point in magnitude
and in principal direction. We hypothesize that a spatially varying fiber angle can
therefore satisfy, \emph{pointwise}, a trade-off between the two energies that a uniform
angle can only satisfy \emph{on average}: turn the fibers across the principal stretch where
stretching energy dominates, and, where bending energy dominates, set them toward or away
from the principal curvature as the frequency constraint requires (away from it when the
frequency is held at the unidirectional value, the lowest of all uniform layups). Stacking separates the same two functionals in the classical way, through
the thickness ($\bm D$ weights the outer plies, $\bm A$ all plies equally
\cite{tsai1968}); it is the competing freedom against which the in-plane route is tested.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} turns the hypothesis into exact statements. It makes five
falsifiable predictions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{P\arabic*},leftmargin=2.2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item An isotropic material offers no orientation lever at all
  (Corollary~\ref{cor:iso}).
\item For a single-ply beam, any uniform angle leaves the frequency-normalized hardening
  \emph{exactly} unchanged, so every gain must come from spatial variation. The
  optimal variation places compliant fibers where the mode does not bend: the inflection
  points (Proposition~\ref{prop:beam}).
\item On a plate, a steered field reaches combinations of linear frequency and hardening
  that no uniform layup reaches, whether a single angle or any stacking sequence.
\item The optimized orientation follows the local preferred directions of the two
  energies. A first-order pointwise design computed from the unidirectional mode alone
  already captures most of the gain (Proposition~\ref{prop:pointwise}).
\item The lever grows with the material anisotropy and is limited by the tow-curvature
  constraint relative to the length scale of the strain fields.
\end{enumerate}
\noindent A consequence follows if the predictions hold. The jump-free amplitude scales
as $J^{-1/2}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:cusp}), so reducing the hardening at held frequency
widens the panel's hysteresis-free operating range by a predictable factor.

\subsection{Approach and findings}
\paragraph{Approach.} We test the predictions on a clamped laminated beam and a simply
supported laminated plate, both von K\'arm\'an. The backbone is computed by an SSM
reduction verified against analytic, harmonic-balance (EPMC) and finite-element
references. For design we use the SSM's hardening coefficient in a closed form that is
exact for the in-plane relaxation and evaluated on a \emph{mode-adaptive} fundamental,
with an analytic adjoint. For the plate the frequency is imposed as an exact equality, so every
reported plate gain is at the stated frequency and fixed mass; for the beam it is a floor
($\omega_1\geq0.98\,\omega_{1,\UD}$). The tow-curvature limit is
imposed pointwise. The held-frequency plate design is confirmed independently on the full
multi-mode model, by its SSM, by EPMC continuation and by direct time integration of
up/down frequency sweeps, and on an independent finite-element model.

\paragraph{Findings in brief.} Four predictions are borne out; the mechanism prediction (P4) holds partly.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{Beam (P1, P2).} Uniform angles leave the hardening invariant to four
  digits. Symmetric steering, with no bending--extension coupling at all, cuts it by
  $\nBInfHeldRed\%$ with the frequency within $\nBeamFreqBand\%$ of the UD value, by placing compliant
  fibers at the inflection points. An optimized bending--extension coupling, the intuitive alternative, gives only $\nBBred\%$ (on its own drift objective).
\item \textbf{Plate (P3).} A curvature-feasible steered tow reduces the
  frequency-normalized hardening to $\nFTen$ of the unidirectional value at
  its frequency (an exact equality in the design model; within $\nHeldTol\%$ on finer bases
  and on an independent finite-element model). No uniform layup can match this: the unidirectional
  plate is the lowest-frequency member of all $\nSnum$ symmetric stacks we enumerate.
  The steered designs also undercut the best stacking sequences at every higher
  frequency we test.
\item \textbf{Mechanism (P4).} The pointwise design computed from the UD fields alone,
  with no optimizer, already gives $J/J_{\UD}=\nStarJ$ (at $\nStarF\,\omega_{1,\UD}$); the
  optimized field shares its large-scale pattern but departs from it by $\nStarDevHigh^\circ$
  RMS where the energies are large. Steering also
  raises the relaxed share of the stretch energy from $\nRelaxUD\%$ to $\nRelaxOpt\%$.
\item \textbf{Limits (P5).} The lever grows monotonically with anisotropy and saturates
  once the minimum tow radius is below about $\SI{20}{mm}$.
\item \textbf{Consequence.} The full-order model confirms the reduction
  (SSM $\nCredSSM\%$), and the jump-free amplitude widens by $\nCacRatio\times$, as
  predicted.
\end{itemize}
