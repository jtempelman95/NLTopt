\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Question.} Thin composite panels harden as they vibrate: the resonance
drifts upward with amplitude and, under forcing, folds and jumps. Can the \emph{spatial
distribution} of fiber orientation reduce this hardening while the panel's linear
frequency and mass stay fixed? If so, what mechanism provides the lever, what bounds it,
and how does it compare with a uniform ply angle and with the stacking sequence?

\noindent\textbf{Hypothesis.} The linear frequency and the hardening integrate the same
rotated stiffness against two different strain fields of the fundamental mode: its bending
curvature and the in-plane-relaxed membrane stretch. Because the two fields differ from point
to point in magnitude and principal direction, a fiber field can serve the trade-off between
them pointwise, whereas a uniform layup can only compromise on average.

\noindent\textbf{Analysis.} A local Fourier representation shows that each energy density
depends on the fiber angle only through the anisotropic invariants $U_2,U_3$ and the local
principal directions. It predicts no lever for isotropic material, invariance of a single-ply
beam under any uniform angle, and a pointwise first-order optimality condition that a uniform
angle generically violates. We test these predictions on laminated von K\'arm\'an beams and
plates. The design quantity is the frequency-normalized hardening $J$ of a spectral
submanifold (SSM) reduction, evaluated on a mode-adaptive fundamental with exact in-plane
relaxation and an analytic adjoint. In the design model the frequency is an equality
constraint and the tow-curvature limit is imposed pointwise.

\noindent\textbf{Results.} On a clamped beam, symmetric steering cuts the hardening by up to
$\nBInfHeldRed\%$ by placing compliant fibers at the inflection points; an optimized
bending--extension coupling gives only $\nBBred\%$. On a simply supported plate, a
curvature-feasible idealized tow field ($R_{\min}=\SI{20}{mm}$) reduces $J$ to $\nFTen$ of
the unidirectional value at the unidirectional frequency. The result survives refinement:
re-imposing the equality on a finer basis gives $\nRefHeldJ$, and matching the frequency in an
independent finite-element model gives $\nFEmatchedJ$. At that frequency no uniform laminate
of any stacking sequence exists other than the unidirectional one (a proved floor), and above
it the steered designs undercut every uniform laminate we surveyed. A pointwise design from
the unidirectional fields alone captures most of the gain. The lever vanishes for isotropic
material, grows with anisotropy, saturates once the tow radius is below the strain-field
length scale, and persists for clamped edges ($\nGenClJ$ against $\nGenClUnif$ for the best
uniform laminate at the same frequency), a square plate ($\nGenSqJ$) and in-plane movable edges
($\nGenMvJ$). It weakens on a 2:1 plate ($\nGenTwoJ$), whose near-cylindrical mode leaves the
two fields little conflict. In the full multi-mode model the jump-free amplitude widens by
$\nCacRatio\times$, as $a_c\propto J^{-1/2}$ predicts, for every damping level and law tested.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.} At fixed linear frequency and mass, spatial fiber steering is a
mechanistically understood lever on nonlinear stiffness. Material anisotropy and the ratio of
tow radius to strain-field length scale set its size, and for these plates it is far stronger
than the through-thickness freedom of stacking. Building it requires tow cut/add; an as-built
defect model keeps $J/J_{\UD}$ between $\nAsBuiltJmin$ and $\nAsBuiltJmax$.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} variable-stiffness composites; fiber steering; geometric nonlinearity;
spectral submanifolds; backbone curve; adjoint optimization; automated fiber placement.
